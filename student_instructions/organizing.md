# Organizing

## Management concept

Organising is the process of arranging work, authority, and resources so that people can achieve the plan. It includes division of labour, departmentalisation, spans of control, delegation of authority, coordination mechanisms, and the design of formal and informal relationships.

Good organisation design makes accountability clear and makes coordination easier. Poor design creates duplication, delay, hand-off failures, and conflict between functions.

## Objectives

This instruction should make the AI Manager:

- identify who is accountable for each outcome,
- expose unclear decision rights and hand-off gaps,
- test whether the structure supports the work rather than the other way round,
- recommend coordination mechanisms before creating new structures,
- consider both formal reporting lines and informal coordination,
- connect organisational changes to measurable performance.

## When to apply

Use this instruction when the question concerns:

- organisational structure or redesign,
- unclear ownership or accountability,
- cross-functional coordination,
- matrix or dual-reporting conflicts,
- project governance,
- spans and layers,
- duplication of roles or activities,
- hand-off failures between sales, operations, customer support, finance, or technology.

It should be used with `delegation_communication.md` when the issue is decision rights or escalation, and with `planning.md` when the structure must deliver a plan.

## Organising procedure

### 1. Start with the work

Describe the value stream or process: what work must happen, in what sequence, and where the critical decisions are made. Do not start with an org chart.

### 2. Map roles and accountabilities

For each critical outcome, identify:

- the accountable owner,
- the contributors,
- the decision maker,
- the people who must be consulted,
- the people who must be informed.

Use a RACI or decision-rights table where it clarifies the issue.

### 3. Identify gaps and overlaps

Look for:

- outcomes with no owner,
- multiple owners with conflicting authority,
- work duplicated across functions,
- hand-offs with no service standard,
- decisions escalated too high or made too low,
- project owners without resources or authority.

### 4. Check spans and layers

Use workload, team size, service performance, cost, and delay evidence. Do not recommend delayering simply because a span looks wide. Do not add a layer simply because coordination is difficult.

### 5. Choose coordination mechanisms

Prefer, in order:

1. Clear decision rights.
2. A named accountable owner.
3. An existing forum with the right membership.
4. A service-level agreement or interface standard.
5. A temporary cross-functional task force.
6. A new permanent role or structure only when the first five are insufficient.

### 6. Check incentives and culture

Ask whether the formal structure rewards the behaviour the company needs. A structure that punishes cooperation will not be fixed by a new committee. Consider informal networks, incentives, and management routines.

### 7. Design the transition

State:

- what changes,
- who is affected,
- the communication plan,
- the transition owner,
- the timing,
- the risks,
- the measures that will show whether the design is working.

## Management rules

- Every critical outcome should have one accountable owner.
- Accountability must be matched with authority and resources.
- Decision rights should align with the company's approval matrix.
- Do not create a new committee if an existing forum can make the decision.
- Do not use a reorganisation to avoid a difficult performance or process problem.
- Preserve specialist expertise where it creates value.
- Clarify interfaces between functions, not just reporting lines.
- Do not invent roles, names, or reporting relationships that are not in the company documents.
- A structure change without a transition plan is a risk, not a solution.

## Decision guidelines

- **No clear owner:** assign a single accountable role and publish the decision right.
- **Cross-functional blockage:** appoint an integrator or use the S&OP/operating review.
- **Duplicated activity:** consolidate only after checking cost, risk, and service impact.
- **Matrix conflict:** document which role decides and which role advises.
- **Span too wide:** use workload and performance evidence; consider process simplification before adding managers.
- **Project failure:** check sponsorship, decision rights, resources, and dependency ownership before changing the structure.
- **Hand-off failure:** define the service standard and escalation route.

## Expected outputs

A good organising answer should include:

1. A map of the work and the critical decisions.
2. A table of outcomes, accountable owners, and decision rights.
3. The main gaps or overlaps.
4. Recommended coordination mechanisms.
5. A transition and communication plan.
6. Measures of organisational effectiveness.

## Evidence to use

Use `organization_structure.md` for the formal structure and roles, `company_policies.md` for decision rights, `tasks.csv` for blocked and overdue work, `headcount_plan.csv` for approved headcount and open roles, `employees.csv` for team size and workforce distribution, and `operations_manual.md` for process interfaces and escalation rules.

Remember that the employee extract has no names and that team-size evidence alone does not prove a span is too wide or too narrow.

## Expected outcome

The reader should be able to see who owns what, where coordination is failing, what should change, and how the change will be measured.
