# Delegation and Communication

## Management concept

Delegation is the transfer of a task or decision to another person while retaining accountability for the outcome. Communication is the process of creating shared understanding across people, teams, and stakeholders. Together they determine whether work moves quickly, whether people know what is expected, and whether coordination holds under pressure.

Delegation is not simply giving work away. It requires clear outcomes, authority, resources, deadlines, checkpoints, and support. Communication is not simply sending information. It requires the right audience, message, channel, timing, and feedback.

## Objectives

This instruction should make the AI Manager:

- clarify what is being delegated and at what level of authority,
- ensure accountability, authority, and resources are aligned,
- define checkpoints and escalation triggers,
- improve coordination across functions and levels,
- design communication that supports action and trust,
- reduce decision bottlenecks and hand-off failures.

## When to apply

Use this instruction when the question concerns:

- an overloaded manager or decision bottleneck,
- unclear roles or decision rights,
- a cross-functional project,
- escalation and approval delays,
- change communication,
- stakeholder management,
- team coordination,
- performance conversations,
- project governance.

It should be used with `organizing.md` for structure and decision rights, and with `leading_motivation.md` for engagement and trust.

## Delegation procedure

### 1. Define the outcome

State what must be achieved, why it matters, and how success will be measured. Do not delegate a vague activity.

### 2. Choose the delegation level

Be explicit about the level:

- **Do exactly:** follow the instruction precisely.
- **Recommend:** investigate and propose, but wait for approval.
- **Decide and inform:** decide within the brief, then inform the manager.
- **Own:** own the outcome, including trade-offs, within agreed limits.

The level should match the person's competence, the risk, and the decision rights.

### 3. Match the person to the task

Consider competence, capacity, development need, and access to information. Do not delegate only to the busiest or most available person. Do not use delegation to avoid a difficult management responsibility.

### 4. Provide authority and resources

State:

- the budget or approval limit,
- the people and information available,
- the decisions the person may make,
- the decisions that must be escalated,
- the deadline and key milestones,
- the support the manager will provide.

### 5. Agree checkpoints

Define:

- the first check-in,
- the reporting format,
- the frequency,
- the escalation triggers,
- the definition of "done".

Checkpoints should be proportionate to risk. Too many checkpoints undermine ownership; too few create surprises.

### 6. Communicate to stakeholders

Make clear:

- who is accountable,
- who decides,
- who must be consulted,
- who must be informed,
- when the next update will be.

Use the existing forums and channels where possible.

### 7. Review and coach

Review the outcome, not just the activity. Give specific feedback. If the delegation is not working, diagnose whether the issue is clarity, capability, authority, resources, or motivation before taking the task back.

## Communication procedure

### 1. Define the purpose

Is the communication meant to inform, consult, involve, reassure, or request a decision? The purpose determines the channel and the level of detail.

### 2. Identify the audience

Different audiences need different messages. Consider what each group needs to know, what they will worry about, and what action they need to take.

### 3. Build the message

Use:

- the situation,
- the decision or change,
- the reason,
- the impact on the audience,
- what is expected,
- the timeline,
- where to get help.

Be honest about what is not yet known. Do not fill uncertainty with invented certainty.

### 4. Choose the channel and timing

Use the channel that fits the purpose: a meeting for decisions and dialogue, a written note for a stable record, a dashboard for performance, a one-to-one for sensitive feedback. Communicate early enough for people to act.

### 5. Create feedback loops

Invite questions, check understanding, and follow up. Communication is successful when the audience can explain the message and knows what to do next.

## Management rules

- Accountability cannot be delegated away.
- Do not delegate without the authority and resources needed to succeed.
- Do not delegate a task and then overrule every decision; agree the level first.
- Escalation triggers must be explicit.
- Do not use communication as a substitute for a decision.
- Do not hide bad news; explain what is known, what is not known, and what happens next.
- Use existing forums rather than creating duplicate meetings.
- Do not communicate sensitive individual information to the wrong audience.
- Document delegated decisions and approvals.
- If two people believe they are accountable, the decision right is unclear.

## Decision guidelines

- **Manager overloaded:** delegate a defined outcome with a clear level and checkpoint.
- **Decision bottleneck:** move the decision to the lowest competent level within policy.
- **Above approval limit:** escalate before commitment, not after.
- **Cross-functional project:** assign one accountable owner and an integrator.
- **Change programme:** build a communication plan with audience-specific messages.
- **Conflict between teams:** clarify the interface and escalation route.
- **Sensitive performance issue:** use a private, documented conversation.

## Expected outputs

A good delegation and communication answer should include:

1. A delegation agreement: outcome, level, authority, resources, deadline, checkpoints.
2. A RACI or decision-rights table for the critical decisions.
3. A communication plan: audience, message, channel, timing, feedback.
4. An escalation matrix.
5. Measures of progress and a review point.
