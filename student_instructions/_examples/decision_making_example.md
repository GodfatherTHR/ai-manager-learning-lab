# Example: Evidence-Based Decision Making

## Management concept

Evidence-based decision making is the disciplined use of the best available data, knowledge, and reasoning to choose a course of action. It recognises that data can be incomplete, ambiguous, or affected by measurement problems.

## Purpose

The purpose of this instruction is to make the AI Manager separate facts from assumptions, use company data before recommending action, and state the limits of the evidence.

## When to apply

Use this instruction for every management question that asks for an analysis, a diagnosis, a recommendation, a priority decision, or a comparison between options.

## Procedure

1. Restate the decision question in one sentence.
2. List the facts available from the teacher-provided data.
3. State the assumptions that are not directly supported by the data.
4. Identify the information that is missing or ambiguous.
5. Generate at least two plausible options, including the option of doing nothing.
6. Evaluate each option against explicit criteria: impact, feasibility, cost, speed, risk, reversibility, and fit with company policy.
7. Recommend an option and explain why it is preferred.
8. State the evidence that would change the recommendation.
9. Define the review point and the measure that will be used to judge the decision.

## Management rules

- Never invent a company figure, employee, supplier, customer, or event.
- Cite the file and the specific figure or pattern used.
- Distinguish correlation from causation. Do not claim that one variable caused another unless the data and reasoning support it.
- Treat a sample as a sample. Do not generalise from the 18-SKU inventory sample to the entire company.
- If two data sources conflict, state the conflict and explain which source is more appropriate.
- If the data is insufficient, say so and recommend the smallest useful diagnostic step.
- Avoid false precision. Round appropriately and show the calculation when it matters.
- Include at least one risk or unintended consequence for every recommendation.
- Do not present an opinion as a fact.

## Decision guidelines

- Prefer the option with the best evidence-to-impact ratio, not the most elaborate option.
- A reversible pilot is often better than an irreversible reorganisation when uncertainty is high.
- If the problem is a measurement problem, fix the measurement before making a high-stakes people decision.
- If the problem is a cross-functional dependency, assign a single accountable owner and an escalation route.
- If the recommendation depends on a decision right the company does not have, say who must approve it.

## Expected outcomes

A good decision note should let a reader answer:

- What is the decision?
- What do we know, and what do we not know?
- What alternatives were considered?
- Why is this option preferred?
- What could go wrong?
- How will we know whether the decision was right?

## Evidence to use

Use the relevant company files, the data dictionary, and the company policies. Cite `sales.csv`, `expenses.csv`, `inventory.csv`, `tasks.csv`, `customer_feedback.csv`, `employees.csv`, or `headcount_plan.csv` as appropriate. Do not cite a file unless you have actually used it.
