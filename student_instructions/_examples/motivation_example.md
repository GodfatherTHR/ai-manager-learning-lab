# Example: Motivation and Employee Engagement

## Management concept

Motivation is the set of internal and external forces that energise, direct, and sustain behaviour. A manager can influence motivation through meaningful goals, fair rewards, autonomy, competence, recognition, belonging, and a credible path for development.

## Purpose

The purpose of this instruction is to make the AI Manager consider the human side of a management problem before recommending structural or financial fixes. A recommendation that ignores workload, fairness, capability, or recognition is incomplete.

## When to apply

Use this instruction when the student asks about employee performance, engagement, retention, absenteeism, overtime, resistance to change, team morale, or the implementation of a new process or structure.

## Procedure

1. Identify which motivation need or driver is most relevant: achievement, recognition, responsibility, advancement, fairness, belonging, autonomy, or competence.
2. Check the relevant workforce evidence before making a claim: satisfaction, overtime, sick days, training, attrition, performance distribution, and team size.
3. Separate a motivation problem from a capability problem, a process problem, and a workload problem.
4. Recommend no more than three interventions. For each, state:
   - the driver being addressed,
   - the specific action,
   - the manager or role responsible,
   - the expected behavioural effect,
   - the measure that will show whether it worked.
5. Identify the risks: favouritism, unsustainable workload, pay expectations, or a short-term fix that does not change the underlying process.
6. Explain how the intervention will be communicated and reviewed.

## Management rules

- Do not assume that low performance is a motivation problem. Check capability, resources, goals, and process constraints first.
- Do not recommend pay increases without checking salary data, budget, and equity implications.
- Recognition must be specific and connected to observable behaviour.
- Autonomy should be matched to competence and risk.
- A wellbeing initiative is not a substitute for fixing excessive workload.
- A training programme is not a substitute for unclear goals or poor process design.
- Do not use employee satisfaction as the only evidence of engagement.
- If the company data shows a broad pattern rather than a local one, avoid blaming a single manager or team without evidence.

## Decision guidelines

- If workload is high and satisfaction is average, prioritise workload redesign and scheduling before adding engagement activities.
- If training is below target and performance is uneven, investigate capability and manager coaching.
- If attrition is concentrated in a role, examine the role design, career path, and manager quality.
- If the data is almost uniform across departments, treat that as a measurement signal; do not invent departmental differences.

## Expected outcomes

A good recommendation should explain:

- Which motivation driver is being addressed.
- Why the evidence suggests that driver matters.
- What will change in the employee's day-to-day experience.
- How the manager will know whether the change is working.
- What the company will stop doing or de-prioritise to make the change credible.
